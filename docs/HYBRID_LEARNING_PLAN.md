# Sarf Mastery: Hybrid Learning & Course Rebuild Plan

**Status:** Active redesign specification  
**Audience:** An absolute beginner learning Arabic morphology (ṣarf), with a traditional Hawza-compatible path  
**Primary language:** English, with carefully reviewed Persian and Urdu support where available  
**Delivery:** Responsive web app for desktop, tablet, and mobile  
**Core promise:** A learner can begin with no knowledge of Sarf, understand what Arabic word forms are doing, and gradually become capable of analysing and producing forms—not merely reciting tables.

---

## 1. The educational problem we are solving

The current application has a useful technical shell and 19 lesson drafts, but a list of lesson titles and short explanations is not yet a complete course. A learner needs a guided experience, enough varied examples to see a pattern, opportunities to listen and respond, explicit teaching at the right moment, feedback that explains why an answer is right, and a planned path from recognition to independent analysis.

The redesign must not simply increase the number of pages. It must increase the amount of **learnable, sequenced, practised, and reviewable teaching** inside each page.

## 2. The hybrid method: acquire first, formalise second

Every new concept is taught through two deliberately connected tracks. They are not competing philosophies and neither is optional.

### Track A — Natural discovery (the child-like path)

The learner meets meaningful Arabic before being asked to memorise a rule.

1. **Hear it:** listen to a word or short phrase, first at natural speed and then slowly where verified audio exists.
2. **See it in context:** see the fully vowelled Arabic, transliteration, plain meaning, and a short context.
3. **Notice:** compare two or more familiar examples. Ask a simple question such as “What stayed the same?” or “What changed?”
4. **Guess:** make a low-stakes prediction. It is acceptable to be wrong; the learner is invited to notice evidence.
5. **Reveal:** explain the pattern in everyday language, using the learner's examples.
6. **Use it:** choose, sort, complete, or produce another example.
7. **Meet it again:** encounter the same pattern in a new root, phrase, listening activity, and later revision.

This track should feel like discovering how a language works—not like reading a grammar reference. Avoid unexplained labels at the beginning of a lesson. Use small, concrete steps and frequent comprehension checks.

### Track B — Explicit Sarf (the conventional path)

After the learner has a concrete mental example, teach the formal account.

1. Give the Arabic technical term with full tashkīl and consistent transliteration.
2. State a precise beginner-friendly definition.
3. Explain the formal rule and its conditions.
4. Show the canonical pattern or conjugation table.
5. Explain how to read the table and how the forms relate to pronouns, tense/aspect, voice, gender, and number.
6. Work through examples step by step.
7. Identify exceptions, limits, and common confusions without overwhelming the beginner.
8. Ask the learner to apply the rule to an unseen example.

### The bridge: always make the connection explicit

Every major concept ends with a “Now name what you noticed” bridge:
- “You noticed that the beginning changed. In Sarf, this prefix is called…”
- “You saw that the root letters remained in the same order. The formal pattern is…”
- “You heard the ending change with the subject. These are the ṣīghahs…”

A learner should never be left wondering why a table or term suddenly appeared. Conversely, the natural-discovery activity must not replace accurate formal instruction.

## 3. Non-negotiable lesson architecture

Every substantial lesson follows this sequence, adapting the number of examples to the difficulty:

1. **Warm-up / retrieval (2–3 min):** one or two questions from earlier lessons.
2. **Listen and look (2–4 min):** a small set of useful, fully vowelled examples with meanings.
3. **Discover the pattern (3–5 min):** compare, sort, predict, and reveal.
4. **Name the idea (3–5 min):** introduce the formal term and definition.
5. **Teach the rule (5–8 min):** stepwise explanation, visual pattern, table, and worked example.
6. **Guided practice (3–5 min):** learner answers with hints available.
7. **Independent practice (3–5 min):** fresh examples without hints.
8. **Use it in context (2–4 min):** short phrase, sentence, or carefully sourced text where appropriate.
9. **Explain it back (1–2 min):** learner says or writes what changed and why.
10. **Review assignment:** a short spaced-recall task due later.

A normal session is approximately 20–30 minutes. Longer paradigms may be split across multiple sessions rather than rushed.

## 4. Teaching principles and safeguards

- Start from zero. Never assume the learner already knows Arabic grammar, terminology, or the Arabic alphabet beyond what the lesson establishes.
- Arabic examples must be checked, correctly spelt, and fully vowelled where the reading is intended to teach pronunciation or morphology.
- Show Arabic, transliteration, and meaning together at beginner level. Transliteration is a support, not a permanent substitute for reading Arabic.
- Distinguish root consonants from short vowels, long vowels, prefixes, suffixes, and pattern letters.
- Teach meanings in context. A root is a family clue, not a single English word that can be mechanically substituted into every derivative.
- Separate morphology (word formation) from syntax (sentence role), lexicon (established word meaning), and tafsīr (interpretation).
- Do not imply that every derived form has one invariant semantic meaning. Teach common tendencies and then show lexical/contextual variation.
- Teach standard paradigms carefully; distinguish regular sound-verb patterns from weak, hamzated, doubled, and other special cases.
- Do not introduce a technical label before it helps the learner describe something they have already seen, except where a minimal label is needed to navigate the course.
- Never fabricate audio, citations, Qur'anic references, hadith attributions, textbook page numbers, or scholarly consensus.
- If verified human audio is unavailable, say so clearly. Browser speech may be offered only as a clearly labelled convenience, never as authoritative Arabic pronunciation.
- Keep English primary and preserve the current option for Persian and Urdu explanations; translations require fluent review before being marked complete.
- Make mistakes productive: feedback explains the reasoning, not just “correct” or “incorrect”.

## 5. A concrete example of the hybrid method

### Concept: the relationship between كَتَبَ, يَكْتُبُ, and كَاتِبٌ

**Natural discovery**
- Present كَتَبَ — kataba — “he wrote”.
- Present يَكْتُبُ — yaktubu — “he writes / is writing”.
- Present كَاتِبٌ — kātibun — “writer”.
- Ask: Which consonants do you recognise in all three? What appears at the beginning of the second word? Which word names a person?
- Let the learner select and compare before revealing the explanation.

**Bridge**
- The consonants ك، ت، ب form the root in these examples.
- The words are not identical: they belong to different word categories and patterns.
- The added material and vowel pattern help signal the form; the meaning of each word is established by Arabic usage and context.

**Conventional explanation**
- Introduce جِذْرٌ (root), وَزْنٌ (pattern), فِعْلٌ (verb), and اِسْمُ الْفَاعِلِ (active participle).
- Show the relevant pattern and explain each component.
- Compare with a second root, such as نَصَرَ / يَنْصُرُ / نَاصِرٌ, while noting that lexical meanings differ.
- Ask the learner to identify root, category, pattern, and meaning in an unseen example.

This sequence gives intuition first and the formal vocabulary needed for Hawza study afterwards.

## 6. Curriculum architecture: 60 guided study days

The existing 8-unit traditional sequence is retained, but its delivery is redesigned around the two tracks. The 19 current lessons are treated as **draft source material**, not as 19 fully completed teaching experiences. Several topics need to be split, expanded, or reordered for a true beginner.

### Unit 1 — Meet Arabic words and discover roots (Days 1–6)
- Day 1: What changes inside a word? Explore familiar words; introduce the purpose of Sarf only after examples.
- Day 2: Word, sentence, and meaning; first encounter with كَلِمَةٌ.
- Day 3: اِسْمٌ، فِعْلٌ، حَرْفٌ through sorting real examples.
- Day 4: Discover the root through a small family such as كَتَبَ، كِتَابٌ، كَاتِبٌ.
- Day 5: Root letters vs vowel marks and pattern material; introduce جِذْرٌ and وَزْنٌ.
- Day 6: Sarf vs Nahw, retrieval practice, and a short diagnostic.
**Mastery:** learner can classify basic word categories, identify a familiar triliteral root, and explain in simple language what Sarf studies.

### Unit 2 — The scale and word structure (Days 7–12)
- Day 7: Why scholars use a scale; discover recurring shapes.
- Day 8: فَاءُ الْفِعْلِ، عَيْنُ الْفِعْلِ، لَامُ الْفِعْلِ and ف ع ل.
- Day 9: Map roots to the scale using sound roots.
- Day 10: Recognise added letters and compare مُجَرَّدٌ / مَزِيدٌ.
- Day 11: Weigh words with more than three letters; practise with varied examples.
- Day 12: Mixed review and a pattern-recognition assessment.
**Mastery:** learner can identify root letters, describe a basic pattern, and distinguish original from added letters in taught examples.

### Unit 3 — Pronouns and the fourteen forms (Days 13–22)
- Day 13: Who is doing the action? Discover person and number in context.
- Day 14: The three groups: غَائِبٌ، مُخَاطَبٌ، مُتَكَلِّمٌ.
- Day 15: Singular, dual, plural; masculine and feminine.
- Day 16: Learn the conventional 14-slot order and why some Arabic forms are shared.
- Day 17–18: Past active, in two manageable passes, with a complete 14-form table.
- Day 19–20: Present active, prefixes and endings, also in two passes.
- Day 21: Past and present passive as meaning contrasts; avoid treating passive as merely a table.
- Day 22: Imperative and prohibitive, addressee forms, and unit assessment.
**Mastery:** learner can navigate the 14 slots, recognise and explain taught active/passive forms, and produce selected forms with support.

### Unit 4 — The six common Abwāb (Days 23–30)
- Day 23: What is a بَابٌ? Hear and compare past/present vowel pairs.
- Days 24–29: One day for each common pattern: نَصَرَ يَنْصُرُ، ضَرَبَ يَضْرِبُ، فَتَحَ يَفْتَحُ، سَمِعَ يَسْمَعُ، كَرُمَ يَكْرُمُ، حَسِبَ يَحْسِبُ.
- Day 30: Mixed listening, sorting, recall, and assessment.
**Mastery:** learner recognises the six taught vowel pairings and understands that the correct باب is often learned from reliable lexical usage, not guessed from an English meaning.

### Unit 5 — Mujarrad, Mazīd, and derived forms (Days 31–38)
- Day 31: Revisit the three-letter base form.
- Day 32: Discover what an added letter does to a word's shape.
- Days 33–36: Common derived forms, grouped by visible pattern and carefully qualified semantic tendencies.
- Day 37: Quadriliteral roots and their basic patterns.
- Day 38: Compare root families and complete a mixed assessment.
**Mastery:** learner identifies taught forms and explains the difference between root and derived pattern without claiming a fixed meaning for every form.

### Unit 6 — Derived nouns (Days 39–45)
- Day 39: Maṣdar as the name of an action or state.
- Day 40: Form I maṣdar variation; learn common examples rather than inventing a universal rule.
- Day 41: اِسْمُ الْفَاعِلِ through people doing actions.
- Day 42: اِسْمُ الْمَفْعُولِ through things receiving actions.
- Day 43: اِسْمُ الزَّمَانِ and اِسْمُ الْمَكَانِ.
- Day 44: اِسْمُ الْآلَةِ.
- Day 45: Build and analyse a same-root family; review.
**Mastery:** learner recognises the taught derived nouns and explains their role and pattern with appropriate caveats.

### Unit 7 — Sound and weak verbs (Days 46–53)
- Day 46: Sound vs weak roots; discover why some forms behave differently.
- Day 47: صَحِيحٌ and the sound-root subtypes.
- Day 48: مِثَالٌ.
- Day 49: أَجْوَفُ.
- Day 50: نَاقِصٌ.
- Day 51: لَفِيفٌ and its subtypes.
- Day 52: مَهْمُوزٌ and مُضَاعَفٌ.
- Day 53: Introductory إِعْلَالٌ، إِبْدَالٌ، إِدْغَامٌ through carefully selected worked examples.
**Mastery:** learner can classify taught root types and follow a worked explanation of common changes.

### Unit 8 — Read, analyse, and teach (Days 54–60)
- Day 54: Guided analysis of familiar words outside the lesson examples.
- Day 55: Short, verified Qur'anic word examples; morphology only, not independent tafsīr.
- Day 56: Carefully sourced hadith/literary examples where attribution and text are verified.
- Day 57: Unseen-word analysis with a structured checklist.
- Day 58: Mixed written assessment.
- Day 59: Oral explanation and recitation of selected paradigms.
- Day 60: Teach-back: explain one word family and one formal rule to a beginner.
**Mastery:** learner can analyse an appropriately levelled unseen word, state uncertainty, and distinguish morphological evidence from contextual interpretation.

## 7. Required content inside the application

### Learn
- Guided lesson path, clear next/previous navigation, estimated time, and visible lesson objectives.
- Distinct “Discover” and “Formal Sarf” sections, visually connected rather than isolated.
- Worked examples with revealable steps.
- A short “Why this matters” section and common beginner questions.

### Listen
- Word, phrase, and conjugation audio where licensed, verified recordings exist.
- Natural and slow playback, repeat, and listen-and-select exercises.
- Audio status must be explicit; do not imply that a disabled or unavailable recording exists.

### Practice
- Recognition, sorting, matching, root extraction, pattern identification, conjugation completion, transformation, and production.
- Hints that can be requested without penalty.
- Explanatory feedback for every answer.
- Gradual reduction of scaffolding.

### Explore
- Searchable word families, roots, patterns, glossary, and cross-links to lessons.
- Each entry distinguishes attested words from illustrative pattern examples.

### Revise
- A short daily review queue using spaced intervals (initially 1, 3, 7, 14, and 30 days).
- Review both meaning/recognition and formal recall.
- Reintroduce errors sooner; do not mark a topic mastered after one correct multiple-choice answer.

### Teach
- Short prompts asking the learner to explain a pattern in their own words.
- Model answer / key points after the learner attempts.
- Optional oral response; no speech-recognition score required for core progression.

## 8. Assessment and mastery

Use three kinds of evidence:
1. **Recognition:** can the learner notice and classify a form?
2. **Recall and production:** can the learner retrieve or complete the pattern without seeing the answer?
3. **Explanation and transfer:** can the learner explain why and apply the idea to a new example?

A topic is provisionally mastered only after success on more than one day and across more than one exercise type. Suggested threshold: at least 80% on a mixed review plus one successful teach-back or worked analysis. This is a learning-design target, not a claim of formal certification.

## 9. Content quality standard

A lesson is not complete merely because a route renders or a paragraph exists. Mark it complete only when:
- [ ] The lesson has the hybrid sequence (discovery → bridge → formal teaching → application).
- [ ] It includes enough varied examples to reveal the pattern (normally 6–10 for a major concept; fewer only when justified).
- [ ] Arabic script, tashkīl, transliteration, and meanings are checked.
- [ ] It contains at least one worked example and one contrast/counterexample.
- [ ] It contains guided practice and independent practice with explanatory feedback.
- [ ] It revisits a prior concept and schedules future revision.
- [ ] Formal rules and paradigms are checked against an identified reliable source.
- [ ] Any Qur'an/hadith use has verified text, attribution, and a clear boundary between morphology and interpretation.
- [ ] Audio is verified or clearly marked unavailable.
- [ ] English is clear; Persian/Urdu are reviewed before being called complete.
- [ ] Desktop, tablet, and mobile layouts work, including Arabic tables and controls.

## 10. Responsive and interaction requirements

- **Desktop:** comfortable reading width; tables can use a wide layout; discovery prompts and formal explanations may sit side-by-side where this improves comprehension.
- **Tablet:** preserve readable Arabic and table labels; use stacked panels when columns become cramped.
- **Mobile:** one-column lesson flow; no horizontal page overflow; tables become labelled cards or a deliberately scrollable table with clear cues; controls are touch-sized.
- All sizes: Arabic tashkīl must not clip; Arabic and Latin direction must remain correct in mixed text; focus, keyboard, contrast, and screen-reader labels must be reviewed.
